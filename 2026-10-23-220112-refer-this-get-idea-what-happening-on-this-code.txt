● This script runs a full pick-and-place demo in MuJoCo with a Franka Panda arm.
  The arm moves to a green cube, grips it, lifts it, carries it 30 cm sideways,
  puts it down and lets go. Four camera feeds are shown in OpenCV windows while
  it runs. There's no learning or vision in the control: the cube's position is
  read straight from the simulator, and a simple inverse kinematics (IK) solver
  moves the arm.

  Overall flow

  load scene ─► find joints/gripper site ─► set start pose ─► read cube position
       ─► build 8 waypoints ─► loop:  check distance → advance state machine
                                      set gripper open/closed
                                      Jacobian → solve for joint step → send
  joint targets
                                      step physics ×5 → render 4 cameras

    and adds a green 8×8×10 cm box with a free joint at (0.8, 0, 0.01), plus

  - dof_ids holds the velocity indices of the 7 arm joints.

  State machine (ik_implemnetion.py:79-122 (ik_implemnetion.py#L79-L122))
  When the gripper site gets within 1 cm of the current target, the code moves
  on to the next state. States 2 and 6 also wait 0.5 s so the fingers have time
  to close or open. data.ctrl[7] drives the finger actuator.

  IK solver, the core of the script (ik_implemnetion.py:125-138
  (ik_implemnetion.py#L125-L138))
  1. mj_jacSite builds the 3×nv position Jacobian J. It maps joint velocities to
     how fast the gripper point moves in x, y and z. This is position only, so
     the gripper's orientation isn't controlled.
  2. Damped least squares: dq = Jᵀ (J Jᵀ + λI)⁻¹ · error. The λ = 1e-3 term
     keeps the arm from making wild moves near singularities, where J Jᵀ is
     nearly impossible to invert.
  3. mj_integratePos adds dq·dt to q_desired, and the 7 values go to
     data.ctrl[:7]. The arm actuators are position servos (kp/kv in
     mjx_panda.xml:262-274
     (mujoco_pick_place/franka_emika_panda/mjx_panda.xml#L262-L274)), so IK only
     picks target joint angles and the PD controllers turn them into torques.

  Each step moves about 1% of the remaining error. Because q_desired keeps
  adding up from itself, if the real arm lags, the target keeps pushing until
  the gripper actually arrives. That gives it some integral action.

  Physics and rendering (ik_implemnetion.py:141-177
  (ik_implemnetion.py#L141-L177))
  - Each 10 ms control step runs 5 physics steps of 2 ms.
  - v.sync() updates the interactive viewer.
  - Four renderers draw the wrist depth view (clipped to 2 m and scaled to
    grayscale), wrist RGB, top RGB and front RGB, and show them with OpenCV.

  Things I noticed in the code

  1. Index mix-up (line 138 (ik_implemnetion.py#L138)): q_desired is a qpos
     vector, but it's indexed with dof_ids, which are velocity indices from
     jnt_dofadr. It only works because the 7 arm hinges come first in both
     vectors. jnt_qposadr is the correct index here.
  2. Finger joints are in the Jacobian: the IK can "use" the finger joints to
     reduce the error, but those values are thrown away. Using only the arm
     columns (jac[:, dof_ids]) would be cleaner.
  3. No orientation control: the wrist can tilt or spin while moving, which can
     make the grasp go wrong.
  4. Tight fit: the cube is 8 cm wide (half-size 0.04) and the gripper opens at

     down.
  5. Waits use wall-clock time: the 0.5 s waits use time.time(). Drawing four
     cameras slows the simulation, so simulation time (data.time) would make the
     waits consistent.
  6. Small cleanups: model.opt.timestep = 0.002 is set every loop, when once
     before the loop would do. It also overrides the scene's 0.005. One Renderer

● Yes, this is doable. I'd split it into two jobs, though: the VLM decides
  where, and the IK you already have handles how. I wouldn't have the VLM
  replace kinematics entirely, at least not at first.

  Three ways to do it

  Approach: A. VLM as perception/planner
  What the VLM outputs: Cube location (pixel or 3D) and which step to do next
  Data needed: A few thousand auto-labeled images
  Fits your 8 GB RTX 4060?: Yes, easily
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Approach: B. VLM outputs waypoints
  What the VLM outputs: The full sequence of gripper positions (the
  pick-and-place plan)
  Data needed: Same as A plus the waypoint plans
  Fits your 8 GB RTX 4060?: Yes
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Approach: C. End-to-end VLA (vision-language-action model)
  What the VLM outputs: Small joint or gripper moves, many times a second
  Data needed: Hundreds to thousands of full demos
  Fits your 8 GB RTX 4060?: Hard. Fine-tuning OpenVLA or pi0 (7B / 3B) won't fit

  comfortably

  I recommend A, then B. The reason is speed. A 2–4B VLM on your GPU takes
  roughly 0.3–1 s per answer, but your control loop runs every 10 ms. Even the
  big VLA labs run the model slowly and leave the fast motor control to
  something else. So the VLM should be called once per phase ("where is the
  cube?", "where should it go?"), and your damped least squares IK should do the
  moving.

  Why this suits your setup

  1. Labels cost nothing. The simulator already knows where the cube is. You can
     put the cube at a random spot, render top_camera, project
     data.xpos[box_id] into pixel coordinates, and save (image, prompt, answer).
     That gives you thousands of perfectly labeled samples in minutes, without
     labeling anything by hand.
  2. Your current script is the expert. For approach B (and C later), the IK
     state machine already produces correct waypoints and trajectories, so you
     can record them as demonstrations.
  3. Your GRPO notebook is a good fit. After supervised fine-tuning (SFT), you
     can use GRPO with a reward like -distance(predicted_point, true_point). A
     smooth reward like this works well with GRPO.

  Concrete pipeline (approach A)

  top_camera RGB ──► fine-tuned Qwen/Gemma ──► {"cube": [u, v], "place": [u, v]}

                depth image / known table height ◄─────┘
                                                       ▼

  - Ask the model for pixel coordinates, not meters. VLMs are good at pointing
    at things in images and bad at estimating 3D distances. Converting pixels to
    3D with the depth image or the known table height is exact.
  - Use a prompt like "Pick up the green cube and place it 30 cm to the left.
    Output JSON." That way the language part matters too. Later you can add
    several colored cubes, so instructions like "pick the red one" make the
    model actually read the instruction.
  - Randomize cube position, color and size, lighting, and camera jitter, so the

  Going further (approach C)

  If you later want true end-to-end control, I'd use LeRobot with SmolVLA

  output actions quickly, they fit in 8 GB, and you'd train them on demos

● Yes, that works. Dropping the depth camera is fine: the table height is fixed,
  and the top and front RGB views together show the cube in 3D. Here's the
  method I'd use.

  Method: the VLM predicts joint-space keyframes

  The VLM outputs the 7 joint angles and the gripper command for the next key
  pose. The arm's position servos then drive to those angles, so no IK runs at
  runtime. IK is only used offline, to make the training labels.

  top RGB + wrist RGB + instruction + current joints + phase
                      │
              fine-tuned Qwen 2B
                      │
        "j1..j7 (degrees), gripper"  ──►  smooth joint interpolation ──►
  position actuators

  Your 8-state machine stays. Each phase calls the VLM once, so there are about
  8 calls per episode. At roughly 0.5–1 s per call on your 4060 that's fine.
  Predicting joints every 10 ms wouldn't be: no 2B VLM is that fast, which is
  why keyframes are the right granularity.

  Example training sample

  [image: top_camera] [image: wrist_camera]
  User: Pick up the green cube. Phase: GRASP.
        Current joints (deg): 0.0 -45.0 0.0 -135.0 0.0 90.0 45.0
  Assistant: 12.4 18.7 -3.1 -121.6 2.0 140.3 54.8 | gripper: open

  Three things that decide whether it works

  1. Use 0.1° precision, not whole degrees. One degree of error at joint 1 moves
     the gripper about 14 mm at your reach. Your cube is exactly as wide as the
     open gripper (8 cm), so that error would make it miss. Use one decimal,
     and I'd also shrink the cube to about 5 cm.
  2. Make the labels consistent. The Panda has 7 joints, so infinitely many
     joint sets put the gripper in the same place. If the labels jump between
     different solutions, the model can't learn them. The fix is to solve IK
     from the same starting pose every time and also hold the gripper pointing
     straight down. That way, nearby cube positions get nearby joint angles.
  3. Close the loop. Each call sees a fresh image and the current joints, so the
     model can correct the previous keyframe's error instead of letting errors
     build up.

  Your GRPO notebook is a good fit here

  After supervised fine-tuning (SFT), you can compute a reward directly in
  MuJoCo:
  - put the predicted joints into qpos, run mj_forward (forward kinematics), and
    measure the distance from the gripper site to the correct target;
  - reward = -distance, plus a bonus for the gripper pointing down and for
    staying within joint limits.

  Each reward check is microseconds of computation and needs no extra labels.
  GRPO then optimizes what you actually care about (where the gripper ends up)
  rather than matching the label angle by angle.

  Plan

  1. Upgrade the expert. Add orientation to your IK (a 6D Jacobian), use a fixed
     starting pose, randomize the cube position, and record the joint angles at
     each of the 8 keyframes.
  2. Generate the dataset. About 3k episodes × 8 keyframes ≈ 24k samples, saved
     as images plus JSONL in your Unsloth Qwen notebook's format.
  3. SFT Qwen 2B with LoRA (fits in 8 GB), then check joint error and
     gripper-position error.
  4. Run it in the sim. A new runner script replaces IK with VLM calls and joint
     interpolation, and we measure grasp success over about 100 random cube
     positions.
  5. GRPO with the forward-kinematics reward to sharpen accuracy.

  Later, you could predict short chunks of dense joint trajectories instead of
  keyframes for smoother motion, but keyframes are the realistic first target.

  Why eval is slow and keeps the CPU at 100%

  Eval generates the answer one token at a time, about 35 tokens per sample.
  Each token is a tiny GPU computation, and Python on the CPU has to launch
  hundreds of small operations for every one of them. So the GPU mostly waits on
  the CPU. Qwen3.5's slow fallback layers (the causal_conv1d_update message)
  make this worse. The result is about 2 s per sample: 300 samples take about 10
  minutes, and the full val set (1,384) would take about 47 minutes.

  This isn't a problem for now. The 10-minute eval at the end is small next to
  2.5 hours of training. The fix is to evaluate 8–16 samples at once, which

● Short answer: I don't think it would increase accuracy. It would probably
  reduce it. Here's why, based on where your base_camera actually is.

  What base_camera sees

  It's mounted on link0 at (0.1, 0, 0.2), only 20 cm above the table, looking
  horizontally forward (+x) with a wide 90° view. So it sees the workspace from
  a low, flat angle.

  Why the top camera is better for this task

  1. Distance is hard to read from a low angle. The joint angles mostly depend
     on the cube's x/y position on the table. From the top camera, both x and y
     map evenly to pixels, at about 2 mm per pixel. From 20 cm high, moving the
     cube 10 cm farther away moves it only a few pixels up in the image, so
     forward/backward position becomes much less precise. That's the error
     you're trying to shrink.
  2. The arm blocks the view. The camera sits under the arm's base, so when the
     arm reaches toward the cube, the forearm and gripper come between the
     camera and the cube, exactly in the phases that matter most.
  3. Objects hide each other. When the cube and pad are lined up in x, the
     nearer one hides the farther one.
  4. Wasted pixels. With a 90° view looking straight ahead, about half the image
     is background above the table.

  When it could help

  - As a third view, alongside top and wrist, it could add a little depth
    information. But each image adds about 140 tokens, so training would be
    roughly 30% slower, and the height information it adds doesn't matter here
    (the table height is fixed).
  - For a real robot, an exact top-down camera is often impractical. A camera
    behind and above the robot, looking down at about 45°, would be the
    realistic choice, and much better than one at 20 cm.

  Cost

  Changing cameras means regenerating the dataset (about 4 minutes) and
  retraining from scratch (about 2.5 hours), because the current model has only
  ever seen top and wrist images.

  My recommendation

  Keep top + wrist for now. Your current errors come from the robot drifting
  into unfamiliar states (the DAgger plan), not from a poor view. If you later
  want to aim for a real robot, we can test a realistic angled camera as a
  separate experiment. The pipeline already supports it (--cams in
  generate_dataset.py, plus a camera added to vla_scene.xml).

  Your running training isn't affected by the new camera in the XML: the dataset
  images are already rendered, and the runner only renders the top and wrist
  cameras.

  Key points:
  - The model gets no coordinates or joint angles. Only the 2 images and the
    phase name go in (that's the image_only style), and 7 joint angles plus a
    gripper command come out.
  - No IK at runtime. The arm goes straight to the predicted angles. IK is only
    used to print the errors.
  - Closed loop: each phase sees fresh images of where the arm actually ended
    up, so the model can correct earlier mistakes. That's why GRASP is sometimes
    more accurate than PRE_GRASP.

● Yes. What you built is a VLA (Vision-Language-Action model) in the basic
  sense: a pretrained vision-language model, fine-tuned so that images + an
  instruction go in and robot actions come out, running in a closed loop. That's
  the same core recipe as RT-2 and OpenVLA. It's a small, simplified version,
  though.

  Same as well-known VLAs

  - It starts from a pretrained VLM (Qwen3.5-2B) and fine-tunes it. It isn't a
    robot network trained from scratch.
  - Actions come out as tokens. RT-2 and OpenVLA also make the language model
    "write" its actions.
  - It's trained on expert demonstrations (behavior cloning), like most VLAs.
  - It runs closed loop: each new action uses fresh camera images.

  Different from well-known VLAs

  simulation. It works and shows the full pipeline (vision → language model →
  joint actions → robot), but it's simpler than research VLAs.

  Steps that would make it more of a "real" VLA

  1. Make the language matter: several cubes in different colors, with
     instructions like "pick the blue one" or "put it on the left pad". This is
     the most important step, because right now the "L" in VLA is barely used.
  2. Let the model decide the phase: remove "Phase 2/8: GRASP" from the prompt,
     so the model works out from the images what to do next.
  3. Act more often: predict small movements every fraction of a second instead
     of 8 big jumps. This needs faster inference (shorter action tokens, a
     smaller model or batching).
  4. Randomize the environment (colors, lighting, floors) so it generalizes.
     Your color tests already showed that's needed.

  1. Exact positions (most important). The model learns a direct mapping from
  pixels to joint angles. If the real camera or robot base sits 2–3 cm or a few
  degrees differently from the sim, every prediction shifts by roughly that
  much. So the sim needs:
  - the robot base position and facing direction relative to the mat;
  - the real cameras' positions, angles and field of view (are photo_cam_1 and

  2. Randomization, so it doesn't overfit to the sim's look. MuJoCo images look
  cleaner than real camera images. Training with small random changes makes the
  model robust to that gap. That includes light brightness and direction, slight
  color and texture shifts of the mat, block and note, small camera position
  jitter of about ±1 cm and ±2°, image noise and blur, and block and note
  positions spread over the whole mat. Your color tests on the Panda showed why

● Short answer: the mat's size matters much less than the camera and robot
  positions, but it can still cost some accuracy. Here's why.

  What the model actually uses. The top camera is fixed relative to the robot,
  so a block at a given pixel always sits at the same spot relative to the robot
  base. That pixel position is what tells the model where to move the arm, so
  in principle it doesn't need the mat at all.

  Why a different mat can still hurt. In the sim, the mat's edges are always in
  exactly the same place in the image. Neural networks like using easy
  landmarks, so the model may have partly learned "the block is X cm from the
  mat edge". If your real mat is bigger, smaller or shifted, those edges move,
  and predictions could be pulled off by some fraction of that difference. It's
  unlikely to be a big error, but it's a risk I can't rule out.

  What matters much more (roughly in order):
  1. Top camera position, angle and field of view relative to the robot base. A
     1 cm camera offset makes the model aim about 1 cm off, and the grasp
     tolerance is only a few mm.
  2. Where the robot base sits relative to the camera. Same effect.
  3. Block size, because it sets the grasp height. Your block is 6.5 cm tall in
     the sim.
  4. Joint calibration, meaning the same angles produce the same pose on the
     real arm.
  5. Mat and table colors and lighting. These are already randomized.

  The mat's size and position come after all of these.

  How to remove the risk:
  - Measure your real mat and put its real size in scene.xml; the sim reads
    everything from that file.
  - Also randomize the mat's size and position in the training data, by about ±5
    cm. The model then can't rely on the edges and has to use the block's
    actual pixel position, so a mat of any size works. It's a small change in
    real_env.py.
